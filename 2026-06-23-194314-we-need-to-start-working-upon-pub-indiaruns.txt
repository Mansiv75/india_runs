❯ wait what vector db are we using ?                                            

● Short answer: none. We're deliberately not using a vector DB.

  Here's the reasoning (it's in STACK.md Section 5 and 8):

  At 100K candidates with 384-dim embeddings, the entire matrix is ~147 MB (100K
  × 384 × 4 bytes). An exact brute-force cosine in numpy over that is
  milliseconds on CPU — jd_emb @ emb.T, one matmul. A vector DB
  (Pinecone/Weaviate/Qdrant/Milvus/FAISS) buys you approximate nearest-neighbor
  speed that we simply don't need at this scale, and every one of them is either
  a network service (violates H1 network-off) or an extra heavy dependency in
  the timed rank.py path.

  So the dense retrieval leg is:
  - Offline: sentence-transformers encodes everything → emb.npy, jd_emb.npy
  (float32, L2-normalized). torch lives here, offline only.
  - Online: load the .npy, exact cosine in numpy, fuse with BM25 via
  reciprocal-rank fusion. No index server, no ANN library.

  There's a subtlety worth naming, since the JD itself lists
  "Pinecone/Weaviate/Qdrant/FAISS in production" as a must-have skill (DESIGN
  4.1 vector_db_or_hybrid_search). That's about what we look for in candidates,
  not what we build the ranker with. Two different things — easy to conflate,
  and a fair question to get at the interview.

  The one caveat already in the doc: FAISS is the documented scale-out path for 
  200K+, where brute-force starts to cost real wall-clock. We note it as the
  upgrade lever but don't pull it into v1 — keeps the runtime lean and H1-safe.

  So if anyone asks at the interview "why no vector DB?", the answer is: exact
  search is faster than the complexity at this scale, and it keeps the timed
  path free of network and heavy deps.

❯ what is a  sandbox framework what is it used for                              

● Good question — "sandbox framework" is two things stuck together, so let me
  unpack both.

  What it is

  In this hackathon it's not a security sandbox. It comes straight from H5 in
  CLAUDE.md:

  ▎ A working sandbox link (HF Spaces / Streamlit / Replit / Colab / public 
  ▎ Docker / Binder) is mandatory and must run the ranker on a ≤100-candidate 
  ▎ sample within the CPU budget.

  So "sandbox" = a publicly hosted, runnable demo of your ranker that a judge
  can open in a browser and execute without installing anything on their own
  machine. The "framework" is just the tool you use to build that web page
  around your code.

  What it's used for

  The judges can't trust a CSV at face value — anyone can hand-craft 100 rows.
  The sandbox is proof the pipeline actually runs and produces those rankings.
  It's the reproducibility check (ties to H6): they click a button, your ranker
  scores a small sample of candidates live, and they see real output. It defends
  against "this was generated by hand / by a hidden LLM call / doesn't actually
  run in budget."

  The frameworks (the "OPEN" choice in STACK.md)

  These are Python libraries that turn a script into a web app with almost no
  web code:

  - Streamlit — you write st.dataframe(results), st.button("Rank"), etc., and it
  renders a web UI. Good for a simple "upload/select a sample → run → show
  ranked table" page.
  - Gradio — similar idea, even less boilerplate for a single input→output demo.
  Pairs natively with HF Spaces.

  And HF Spaces (Hugging Face Spaces) is the hosting — a free CPU server that
  runs your Streamlit/Gradio app at a public URL. So the full picture is: HF 
  Spaces (host) + Streamlit or Gradio (the UI framework) wrapping rank.py (your 
  actual code).

  What ours specifically needs to do

  A minimal page that:
  1. loads the small sample (≤100 candidates) and the precomputed artifacts,
  2. runs rank.py on CPU within the ≤5-min / 16-GB budget,
  3. displays the top-100 table — candidate_id, rank, score, reasoning.

  That's it. It's a thin wrapper over the ranker, not new logic — which is
  exactly why we deferred picking Streamlit vs Gradio until we build the demo.
  The ranking engine doesn't care which one we choose.

❯ what do you recxommend ?                                                      

● Gradio for this specific job.

  Here's the reasoning, tied to what our demo actually needs:

  Our sandbox is a thin wrapper — pick a sample, run rank.py, show a 100-row
  table. That's a single input→output flow, which is exactly Gradio's sweet
  spot. The deciding factors:

  1. Native HF Spaces fit. Gradio is made by Hugging Face, so on Spaces it's the
  path of least resistance — less config, fewer surprises, more deployment
  examples to crib from. Since HF Spaces is our host, this matters.
  2. Less boilerplate for input→output. gr.Interface(fn=run_ranker, inputs=..., 
  outputs=gr.Dataframe()) is close to the whole app. Streamlit's
  rerun-on-every-interaction model means you fight st.session_state and caching
  to stop the ranker re-running on every widget tweak — avoidable friction for a
  one-shot demo.
  3. It's a demo, not a dashboard. Streamlit wins when you want lots of
  interactive widgets, charts, exploration. We don't — we want "click, run, see
  the table." Gradio's narrower scope is an advantage here.

  When I'd flip to Streamlit: if we decide the sandbox should double as an
  exploration tool for the interview — e.g. toggle weight sliders, show the
  feature breakdown per candidate, eval-harness charts. Streamlit is nicer for
  that richer, multi-widget story. If the sandbox is purely the H5 "proof it
  runs" artifact, Gradio is the leaner choice.

  My call: Gradio, unless you want the sandbox to also be an interactive talking
  point at the Stage-5 interview — then Streamlit. Want me to lock Gradio in
  the STACK.md decision log, or leave it OPEN until we see whether we want the
  interactive-exploration angle?

● Dropping operational concerns — pure algorithmic complexity. N = number of
  vectors, d = dimensions.

  The three index types, in plain terms

  Flat (brute force) — compare the query against every vector, keep the best. No
  index, no shortcuts.
  - Analogy: reading every book in the library to find the one you want.
  - Query: O(N·d) — linear in the number of candidates. Exact, 100% recall.
  - Build: nothing (O(1)).

  IVF (Inverted File) — first cluster all vectors into nlist buckets (via
  k-means). At query time, only scan the nprobe buckets whose centers are
  closest to the query, ignore the rest.
  - Analogy: the library is sorted into sections; you only walk the 2–3 shelves
  most relevant to your topic instead of all of them.
  - Query: O(nlist·d) to pick the nearest buckets + O((nprobe/nlist)·N·d) to
  scan inside them. With nprobe ≪ nlist, that second term is a fraction of N —
  sublinear in practice.
  - Build: k-means training, O(N·d) (times a few iterations).
  - Approximate: if the best match sits in a bucket you didn't probe, you miss
  it. More nprobe → higher recall, slower.

  HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) — build a multi-layer graph of
  vectors connected to their neighbors; top layers are sparse "highways," lower
  layers dense "local roads." Query starts at the top, greedily hops to closer
  and closer neighbors, descending layers.
  - Analogy: a highway network — take the expressway to the right city, then
  local streets to the exact address. Or a skip-list for vectors.
  - Query: O(log N · d) — the big win; barely grows as N explodes.
  - Build: O(N·log N · d) — expensive, and the graph edges cost extra memory
  (~1.5–2× the vectors).
  - Approximate, but typically very high recall (95–99%).

  Side by side

  ┌──────┬──────────────┬────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────┐
  │      │    Build     │             Query              │      Recall      │
  ├──────┼──────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────┤
  │ Flat │ O(1)         │ O(N·d)                         │ exact (100%)     │
  ├──────┼──────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────┤
  │ IVF  │ O(N·d)       │ ~`O((nprobe/nlist)·N·d)` —     │ approximate,     │

❯ what is MAcs what is blas ?                                                   

● MACs and BLAS — both came up when I said brute-force search is "38M MACs via
  BLAS, ~milliseconds." Here's what each means.

  MAC — Multiply-Accumulate

  A MAC is one multiply-then-add operation: result = result + (a × b). It's the
  atomic unit of work in almost all numerical/ML compute.

  Why it's the natural unit here: comparing two vectors by cosine/dot-product
  means multiplying them element-by-element and summing:

  dot(q, v) = q[0]·v[0] + q[1]·v[1] + ... + q[383]·v[383]

  For d = 384 dimensions, that's 384 multiply-adds = 384 MACs for one vector
  comparison. Do that against all N = 100K candidates:

  100,000 vectors × 384 dims = 38,400,000 MACs ≈ 38M MACs per query

  So "38M MACs" is just "38 million multiply-add operations" — my way of
  counting the actual arithmetic in one brute-force search. Hardware specs
  (CPUs, GPUs) are often rated in FLOPs, and 1 MAC ≈ 2 FLOPs (one multiply + one
  add), so 38M MACs ≈ 76M FLOPs. A modern CPU does billions of FLOPs per
  second, which is why 76M finishes in milliseconds.

  BLAS — Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms

  BLAS is a standardized, decades-old set of highly optimized routines for
  vector and matrix math — dot products, matrix-vector multiply, matrix-matrix
  multiply. It's the engine under the hood of numpy, PyTorch, R, MATLAB,
  basically all numerical computing.

  The point: you don't write the 38M-MAC loop yourself in Python (which would be
  slow). When you call jd_emb @ emb.T in numpy, numpy hands it to a BLAS
  library — concrete implementations include OpenBLAS, Intel MKL, or Apple
  Accelerate — written in tuned C/Fortran/assembly that:

  - uses SIMD (does several multiply-adds in one CPU instruction),
  - uses multiple cores,
  - is cache-aware (arranges memory access so the CPU isn't waiting on RAM).

  So a brute-force search that looks like a tiny one-line numpy operation is
  actually BLAS executing those 38M MACs at near-peak hardware speed. That
  combination — small arithmetic count (38M) run through a maximally-tuned
  library (BLAS) — is exactly why I said exact search at 100K is milliseconds
  and we don't need an ANN index.

  One-line summary: a MAC is the single multiply-add that vector comparison is
  made of; BLAS is the optimized library that runs millions of them per
  millisecond. They're the why behind "brute force is fast enough at our scale."

❯ what is recall and precision in simple terms                                  

● Recall and Precision are two ways of measuring "did I find the right things?"
  — they answer different questions.

  The core idea

  Imagine there are 10 truly good candidates hidden in the pool, and your system
  returns a list of results.

  - Precision = of the things I returned, how many were actually good?
  → "How clean is my list?" (Did I waste slots on junk?)
  - Recall = of all the good things that exist, how many did I find?
  → "How complete is my catch?" (Did I miss any?)

  Formulas, in plain words:
  Precision = (good ones I returned) / (total I returned)
  Recall    = (good ones I returned) / (total good ones that exist)

  A concrete example

  Pool has 10 truly good candidates. Your system returns 20 results, and 8 of 
  them are actually good.

  - Precision = 8 returned-good / 20 returned = 40% — less than half my list is
  gold.
  - Recall = 8 found / 10 that exist = 80% — I caught most of the good ones, but
  missed 2.

  The fishing-net analogy

  You're fishing for tuna.
  - High recall = a huge net — you catch almost every tuna in the sea, but also
  haul in junk (low precision).
  - High precision = a tiny, careful net — everything you catch is tuna, but you
  miss most of them (low recall).

  They trade off: casting wider improves recall but usually hurts precision, and
  vice versa. That tension is why both are reported together.

  Where this shows up in our project — two distinct places

  1. ANN recall (the vector-index discussion):
  When I said HNSW has "95–99% recall," it means: of the true nearest neighbors
  brute-force would find, the approximate index finds 95–99% of them. Flat/numpy
  is 100% recall because it checks everything — that's the whole reason we
  don't lose anything by skipping ANN at our scale.

  2. Ranking quality (the scoring metrics in CLAUDE.md):
  The hackathon scores us partly on P@10 — Precision at 10 = of your top 10
  ranked candidates, how many are genuinely good fits. That's precision measured
  on just the top slice of the list.

  There's also a related cousin, NDCG, which is precision-flavored but
  order-aware — it rewards putting the best candidates at rank 1–2, not just
  somewhere in the top 10.

  Why the asymmetry matters for our design

  Our pipeline is two stages, and each stage cares about a different one:

  - Stage 1 (retrieve the shortlist of ~1000–2000): optimize for recall. We must
  not drop a good candidate here — once they're out of the shortlist, the
  reranker can never recover them. A wide net is correct. (This is exactly the
  "recall guard" in DESIGN 9.1 — we set K generously so plain-language good
  candidates don't get missed.)
  - Stage 7 (the final top-100, especially the top-10): optimize for precision.
  Now every slot is precious; we want the list clean and the very best at the
  top, because the scoring weights top-10 quality heavily.

  So the design deliberately runs recall-first, then precision-first — cast a
  wide net, then carefully sort the catch. That's the natural shape whenever you
  can't afford to either miss good things early or show junk at the end.
